Rationale

Why Another Framework?

Most AI governance approaches are either too abstract — a list of principles that never translate into action — or too technical — accessible only to IT and legal teams. The result: organisation-wide adoption fails and compliance remains on paper.

ORIENT was designed to close this gap. Every stage is concrete, actionable, and sector-agnostic.

"Compliance is an orientation, not a checklist."
— Hexis, inspired by the concept of ἕξις

In ancient Greek, ἕξις (hexis) denotes a stable disposition acquired through repetition and practice — a way of acting that becomes second nature. AI governance works the same way.

Framework

Six Stages, One Cycle

Each stage builds on the previous one, and the last stage feeds back into the first — because AI governance is not a project, it is a process.

O
Stage 1 — Observe

Map Your AI Systems

You cannot manage a risk you cannot see. This stage begins by making all AI use visible — purchased tools, API integrations, internal models. Which decisions does a human make, and which does a model make? Where does data come from, and where does it go?

Many organisations skip this stage and go straight to writing compliance documentation. In ORIENT, without observation, no subsequent stage rests on solid ground.

Key questions
  • How many AI tools are actively used in your organisation?
  • Which of these influence human decisions?
  • Which data crosses organisational or national boundaries?
  • Have reasonably foreseeable misuse scenarios been identified?
EU AI Act
Article 9 mandates a risk management system — but risk management cannot begin without first defining the system. The Observe stage is the practical starting point for Article 9 compliance.
KVKK
Overlaps with KVKK's data inventory obligations (Art. 10–12). Organisations that have already conducted a KVKK compliance process may consider this stage partially complete.
Hexis
AI system mapping template, data flow diagram guide, and inventory methodology.
ISO 42001 Cl. 6.1 EU AI Act Art. 9 KVKK Art. 10–12
R
Stage 2 — Risk

Classify and Assess

Your map is ready; now it is time to understand how much risk each AI system carries. The EU AI Act introduces a risk-based classification system: unacceptable, high, limited, minimal. This classification determines which obligations apply.

Risk assessment is not a document completed once and filed — it must be repeated whenever a new system is deployed or an existing system is updated.

Key questions
  • Which AI systems influence decisions about employees, customers, or citizens?
  • Do you use AI in biometrics, credit, employment, or healthcare?
  • Is the risk classification documented?
EU AI Act
Articles 6–9 set out mandatory requirements for high-risk systems. Incorrect classification leads either to unnecessary compliance costs or to insufficient risk coverage.
KVKK
KVKK's Data Protection Impact Assessment obligation (Art. 12) largely overlaps with the EU AI Act's high-risk system assessment. The two processes can be managed in an integrated way.
Hexis
Sector-specific risk classification matrix, integrated DPIA × AI Act assessment template, and expert guidance.
EU AI Act Art. 6–9 ISO 42001 Cl. 6.1 KVKK Art. 12
I
Stage 3 — Identify

Map Legal Obligations

The risk level is established; now the applicable legal obligations must be clearly identified. Which EU AI Act articles apply? Is the organisation a provider or a deployer? What overlapping obligations arise from KVKK?

Without the Identify stage, Evaluate is meaningless — you cannot measure your current position without knowing what you are required to meet.

Key questions
  • Is your organisation a provider or a deployer of this AI system?
  • Which EU AI Act articles and Annex III categories apply?
  • Have overlapping KVKK obligations been identified?
EU AI Act
Article 6 and Annex III define high-risk system categories. Article 2 scope definition — provider, deployer, extraterritorial effect — is the starting point for identifying obligations.
KVKK
KVKK Art. 5–6 data processing conditions overlap with EU AI Act Art. 10 data governance obligations. For Turkish organisations, dual compliance mapping is produced at this stage.
Hexis
Provider / deployer determination, applicable article list, and KVKK × EU AI Act overlap map.
EU AI Act Art. 2, 6 Annex III KVKK Art. 5–6
E
Stage 4 — Evaluate

Assess Current Controls

Obligations are identified; now it is time to compare existing policies, technical measures, and processes against those obligations to identify compliance gaps. The output of the Evaluate stage is not a failure list — it is a clear baseline assessment that feeds prioritisation.

This stage is not a "failure test" — it is an opportunity to see the organisation's real position. Identifying gaps now means correcting them before an enforcement process begins.

Key questions
  • Which obligations do your current controls meet, and which do they not?
  • Has a priority order been established among identified gaps?
  • Does the assessment include an independent review?
EU AI Act
Article 9 requires continuous evaluation within the risk management system. Article 43 conformity assessment requires the current state to be documented as a precondition.
KVKK
KVKK Art. 12 technical and administrative measures obligation overlaps with EU AI Act Art. 9 control requirements. A single assessment process can satisfy both obligations.
Hexis
Compliance gap analysis template, control maturity assessment, and prioritisation matrix.
EU AI Act Art. 9, 43 ISO 42001 Cl. 8.4 KVKK Art. 12
N
Stage 5 — Navigate

Plan Your Compliance Path and Prepare for Deployment

Compliance gaps are identified; now it is time to build a prioritised roadmap to close them. The Navigate stage translates findings into action: which step comes first, which resource goes where, which timeline is realistic.

But completing the compliance path is not enough. Before a system goes live, readiness must be confirmed across five dimensions: technical, governance, legal, operational, and human oversight. Without this confirmation, a deployment decision cannot be made.

Key questions
  • Have compliance gaps been listed in priority order?
  • Has technical and governance readiness been confirmed before deployment?
  • Are human oversight mechanisms defined and tested?
EU AI Act
Articles 9 and 17 — risk management system and quality management system requirements mandate documentation of the compliance process and pre-deployment control mechanisms.
KVKK
KVKK Art. 12 technical and administrative measures align with the Navigate stage's readiness confirmation process.
Hexis
Prioritised remediation roadmap, Release Readiness checklist, and responsibility matrix.
EU AI Act Art. 9, 17 ISO 42001 Cl. 8 KVKK Art. 12
T
Stage 6 — Track

Monitor Continuously and Adapt

AI systems change, regulations evolve, and an organisation's AI use expands. Governance must therefore be a continuously monitored and updated practice, not a structure built once and left in place.

The output of the Track stage feeds back into the Observe stage — the cycle closes. This cyclical nature is the defining characteristic that distinguishes ORIENT from linear compliance frameworks.

Key questions
  • Who monitors the performance and drift risk of your AI systems?
  • How quickly can you detect and implement new regulatory changes?
  • Do you have an annual AI governance review process?
EU AI Act
Articles 72–73 introduce post-market monitoring and incident reporting obligations. Without a continuous monitoring infrastructure, meeting these obligations becomes reactive and costly.
KVKK
Periodic compliance review (KVKK Art. 12) and the EU AI Act's continuous monitoring requirements can be combined into a single review cycle.
Hexis
Periodic AI governance review template, regulatory change tracking, and maturity measurement tool.

International Alignment

Why This Sequence?

ORIENT's six stages are not arbitrary. Every major international AI governance framework follows the same logic: first understand, then measure, then decide, then act, then monitor. ORIENT expresses this shared logic in a single process — practical and accessible for organisations without dedicated AI governance teams.
