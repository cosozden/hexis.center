**Q1.** [Domain II.C | Bloom: Apply] ⚠️ CLOSE-PAIR

A European manufacturer develops an AI system that analyzes real-time sensor data from factory equipment to predict mechanical failures and automatically schedules preventive maintenance. The system operates exclusively within the company's own factories and does not interact with any product placed on the market. The manufacturer classifies the system as "minimal risk" because it is an internal operational tool rather than a product safety component.

A compliance consultant reviews this classification. Under the EU AI Act, which statement CORRECTLY evaluates the manufacturer's reasoning?

A) The classification is correct, because the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions under Article 6(1) apply exclusively to AI systems that are themselves placed on the market as standalone products — internal operational tools used solely within the developer's own facilities fall outside the scope of Annex I product safety legislation

B) The classification requires further analysis, because if the factory equipment constitutes machinery covered by Annex I product safety legislation, the AI system may qualify as a safety component of that machinery under Article 6(1) — regardless of whether the AI system itself is separately placed on the market

C) The classification is correct, because Article 6(1) only applies when the AI system requires a third-party conformity assessment under the applicable product safety legislation listed in Annex I — internal systems used by the manufacturer are exempt from third-party assessment requirements

D) The classification requires further analysis, because all AI systems used in industrial manufacturing environments are automatically classified as high-risk under Annex III, Area 7 (critical infrastructure) — the manufacturer's internal-use argument is irrelevant to the risk classification

**Q2.** [Domain II.A | Bloom: Analyze] — Case Study

A multinational bank headquartered in Germany deploys an AI-powered credit scoring system that processes applications from customers across the EU. The system uses a fully automated decision-making process with no human intervention. A French applicant whose loan is denied requests an explanation of the decision logic. The bank responds that it can only provide a general description of the factors considered (income, employment history, credit history) but cannot reveal the specific weightings or algorithmic logic because this constitutes a trade secret.

Under GDPR provisions relevant to automated decision-making, which assessment is MOST accurate?

A) The bank must provide meaningful information about the logic involved, the significance, and the envisaged consequences of the processing for the data subject — the trade secret defense does not eliminate the obligation but may influence the form and level of detail of the explanation provided

B) The bank's position is legally sufficient, because GDPR Article 22 requires only "meaningful information about the logic involved" — which courts have consistently interpreted as requiring disclosure of the categories of data used, not the proprietary algorithmic weightings or model architecture

C) The bank is in full compliance because GDPR does not apply to credit scoring decisions — financial services automated decisions are governed exclusively by the Consumer Credit Directive, which contains its own separate transparency requirements that take precedence over GDPR

D) The bank must disclose the complete algorithmic source code and all feature weightings to the applicant, because GDPR's transparency principle requires full technical disclosure whenever automated decisions produce legal effects — trade secret protections are explicitly overridden by data subject rights
